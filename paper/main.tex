\begin{abstract}
Client diversity is often cited as the Lightning Network's defence against a bug in any one implementation, yet
LND runs 84\% of public nodes and holds 84\% of public channel capacity, a share that has barely moved since 2023.
We test what diversity buys with a natural experiment. On 26~August~2026, amid security fixes in every
implementation, Core Lightning (CLN) maintainers told operators who could not upgrade to disconnect their nodes.
Using 805 daily gossip snapshots, 132,360 on-chain channel closes and a client classifier validated against
self-identifying aliases and against the wallet that builds each node's funding transactions, we find that 22\% of
CLN nodes went dark overnight, ten times the usual rate, mostly Tor-reachable ones; a third were still dark
five weeks later. The rest of the network barely noticed. Reachability among all node pairs fell by 7.8
percentage points, a loss exceeded in 4 of 94 ordinary two-day windows and half that of a Tor denial-of-service
wave in July, while among CLN pairs it fell by 24.5 points, more than in any ordinary window. Calibrated failure
scenarios explain the containment: the blast radius depends on whether a failure reaches the hubs, not on how many
nodes run the failing software. Losing ACINQ's Eclair node alone costs the surviving network four times the reachability
lost with all 619 routable CLN nodes, and minority clients' reliance on LND for connectivity is what their size
implies; CLN nodes in fact peer with each other more than chance. Diversity statistics that count nodes mislead;
the quantity to track is which software the hubs run.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Lightning Network (LN)~\cite{poon2016bitcoin} routes Bitcoin payments over a network of two-party payment
channels. Its protocol is defined by the Basis of Lightning Technology (BOLT)
specifications~\cite{bolt2,bolt3,bolt7,bolt9}, and four independent implementations---LND (Lightning Network
Daemon), Core Lightning (CLN), Eclair and LDK (Lightning Development Kit)---interoperate on the same public
network. Client diversity is commonly invoked as a safety property: a bug in one implementation should not take
down the network. The public network does not look diverse. LND runs 84\% of public nodes and holds 84\% of public
capacity, and its capacity share has stayed between 84\% and 90\% for three years. Does the remaining diversity
protect anything, and is the concentration the risk it appears to be? We study the public (announced) graph, the
part of the network that forwards payments for others; wallets on unannounced channels attach to it through their
providers' nodes and are not visible in gossip.

Both questions have been hard to answer because client-correlated failures had not been observed. Topological
studies~\cite{rohrer2019discharged,seres2020topological,lin2020lightning,martinazzi2020evolving,lee2020robustness}
remove random or highly connected nodes from a snapshot; removing ``all LND nodes'' from a graph in which LND holds
85\% of capacity is trivially catastrophic and says little about realistic failures, which are partial,
version-scoped and temporary. In 2026 a natural experiment arrived. Between July and October every implementation
shipped security fixes, for different bugs, and on 26~August CLN maintainers told operators who could not upgrade
to take their nodes off the network. A sizeable cohort of one client went dark at once, in production. We measure
what happened and use it to calibrate a model of client-correlated failure.

The answer is that the outage hurt CLN's own users and almost nobody else. The nodes that complied were mostly
Tor-reachable nodes at the periphery of the graph, and the network as a whole lost about as much
reachability as in an unusual but ordinary two days. What determines the blast radius of a correlated failure is
whether it reaches the hubs: a single Eclair node, ACINQ's, matters more to everyone else than the entire CLN
cohort, and a transport-level failure (a denial-of-service wave against Tor in July) cost the network
twice as much reachability as the CLN shutdown. Counting nodes per client is the wrong way to assess diversity.

\paragraph*{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1: Validated client inference.} Feature-bit rules that stay valid on historical snapshots are
checked against 795 self-identifying aliases and against an independent on-chain signal, the wallet that builds a
node's channel funding transactions (\S\ref{sec:inference}).
\item \textbf{C2: Three years of concentration.} LND's share of public capacity stayed between 84\% and 90\% from
May~2023 to October~2026; apparent changes in node shares coincide with a change in our gossip vantage point
(\S\ref{sec:concentration}).
\item \textbf{C3: An observed client-correlated outage.} We measure the CLN shutdown against churn baselines and a
control client: who complied, how long nodes stayed dark, how peers and the closing market reacted, and how the
event differs from transport failures and single-operator closing waves that resemble incidents
(\S\ref{sec:outage}).
\item \textbf{C4: What decides the blast radius.} Calibrated on C3, compared with 94 ordinary two-day windows and
with targeted baselines, and tested against a null model of how clients connect, client-correlated failures stay
contained; hubs, not cohorts, carry the risk (\S\ref{sec:resilience}).
\end{itemize}
All data are public gossip and blockchain data; code, aggregate datasets and every number quoted in the text are
released (see Data and Code Availability).

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph*{Gossip and channel state}
Nodes announce themselves (\texttt{node\_announcement}, including a feature-bit vector~\cite{bolt9}) and the
routing policy of each channel direction (\texttt{channel\_update})~\cite{bolt7}. A \texttt{channel\_update} can
mark its direction \emph{disabled}; implementations do so when the peer has been unreachable for some minutes.
The volume and propagation delay of such updates have been measured~\cite{gogge2023convergence}. A node that is
offline cannot announce anything; its \emph{peers} disable their direction toward it. Outages are therefore visible
as peer-side disabled flags, which is how we measure them. Node availability and reliability have also been
studied empirically~\cite{waugh2020availability}.

\paragraph*{Closing channels on-chain}
A channel is funded by a 2-of-2 output. A cooperative close spends it with a transaction both parties sign; a
unilateral (force) close broadcasts the latest commitment transaction, recognizable by its encoded commitment
number in \texttt{nLockTime} (upper byte \texttt{0x20}) and the input \texttt{nSequence} (upper byte
\texttt{0x80})~\cite{bolt3}. Its outputs are later swept (delayed \texttt{to\_local}, \texttt{to\_remote},
anchors, and the outputs of in-flight hash time-locked contracts, HTLCs) or, if a revoked state was published, claimed by the counterparty with a \emph{justice}
transaction. Prior work classified closes on-chain~\cite{grotschla2025lifecycle} and by implementation
pair~\cite{zabka2022empirical}, and linked on-chain and gossip data~\cite{kappos2021empirical,romiti2021crosslayer}.

\paragraph*{Resilience and attacks}
Rohrer et al.~\cite{rohrer2019discharged}, Seres et al.~\cite{seres2020topological}, Martinazzi and
Flori~\cite{martinazzi2020evolving} and Lee and Kim~\cite{lee2020robustness} found the LN robust to random
failures and fragile to the targeted removal of central nodes; Lin et al.~\cite{lin2020lightning} documented its
centralisation and showed that removing hubs splits it into many components. Attack studies exploit HTLC
handling, congestion, balance locking and correlated mass exits
\cite{harris2020flood,mizrahi2021congestion,perez2020lockdown,sguanci2023massexit}, and implementation defaults
shape the cost of some attacks~\cite{tikhomirov2020quantitative}. None conditions failure on the implementation or
observes one.

\paragraph*{Client inference}
Zabka et al.~\cite{zabka2021node,zabka2022empirical} infer implementations from default channel-policy and
node-announcement values; Espinasa-Vilarrasa et al.~\cite{espinasa2024decision} use BOLT~\#9 feature bits and
encrypted traffic metadata. We use feature bits, validate them against an on-chain signal, and keep the rules
valid across three years of feature-set changes.

\paragraph*{Monoculture and client diversity}
That software monoculture concentrates risk is a classic argument in security~\cite{geer2003cyberinsecurity}.
Birman and Schneider~\cite{birman2009monoculture} counter that the risk is often overstated: diversity multiplies
the distinct attacks available, and independently built implementations of one specification can share
vulnerabilities. In blockchains, client bugs have caused consensus failures, from Bitcoin's 2013 chain
fork~\cite{bip50} to Ethereum client incidents~\cite{prysm2023finality}, motivating differential testing across
clients~\cite{yang2021fluffy} and incentives for diversity~\cite{ron2026clientdiversity}. Lightning differs: a
client failure takes nodes offline rather than splitting consensus, so its cost depends on where in the payment
graph the failing nodes sit, which is what we measure.

\section{Data and Vantage Point}
\label{sec:data}

All inputs are truncated at a data cutoff of 4~October~2026.

\paragraph*{Daily gossip}
Our LND node dumps its view of the public graph (\texttt{describegraph}) daily. Between 8~May~2023 and the cutoff
(1,246 calendar days), 857 dumps produced usable extracts; the other days are missing, mostly in 2023 and early
2025, or were error responses from LND (``wallet locked'' after restarts). A quality pass excluded 51 partial dumps
(May--June~2024) and one stale day, leaving 805 days (65\% of the calendar). Coverage is complete enough for daily
analysis from May~2026, the window of \S\ref{sec:outage}. The node was replaced in mid-2024, which changed the
view: before, about 42k channels and 8.4k nodes with channels; after, 32--48k channels (median 41k) and 10--13k
nodes. We treat the two periods as \emph{vantage epochs} and compare levels only within an epoch. Gossip is a single
node's view of the public (announced) graph; unannounced channels, where much LDK (wallets) and Eclair (Phoenix)
usage lives, are not visible.

\paragraph*{On-chain closes and spends}
Our own Bitcoin Core node scans every block since January~2025 for spends of the 257,363 funding outputs of every
channel ever seen in our gossip. For each close we record the transaction, block time and type (cooperative, force,
splice); for each output of a force close, the transaction that spends it and its type from the witness script. The
data contain 132,360 closes (99,016 cooperative, 33,321 force, 23 splices) and 112,837 spends of force-close
outputs. Batched sweeps' fees are split across their inputs.

\paragraph*{Funding transactions}
For the 226,134 funding transactions of channels in our catalogue we record the funder wallet's construction
choices: \texttt{nVersion}, \texttt{nLockTime} policy, input \texttt{nSequence}, input and change script types,
output ordering, signature grinding, fee rate.

\section{Client Inference and Validation}
\label{sec:inference}

\paragraph*{Rules}
Implementation-specific feature bits decide most nodes. Bits 2023 (script-enforced lease) and 31 (AMP, atomic
multi-path payments), which LND
assigns outside BOLT~\#9, appear only on LND. Bit 37 (\texttt{option\_attribution\_data}), or 29
(\texttt{option\_dual\_fund}) together with 61 (\texttt{option\_simple\_close}), marks Eclair. Bit 11
(\texttt{gossip\_queries\_ex}) together with 39 (\texttt{option\_onion\_messages}) or 43
(\texttt{option\_provide\_storage}) marks CLN or Eclair, which we separate by bit 55 (keysend): CLN advertises it
by default (about 96\% of CLN-set nodes), Eclair does not. Bits 39, 43 or 63 (\texttt{option\_splice}) without 11
mark LDK. Fallbacks (LND's default colour and alias, and the modal default CLTV delta, the number of blocks of
CheckLockTimeVerify time-lock a node requires to forward a payment) yield low or medium confidence.
Operators with a publicly known implementation (ACINQ, Block) are attributed directly. An earlier version without
the keysend rule labeled ACINQ as CLN before 2025, when Eclair added bits 37 and 61: rules must be validated against
historical snapshots, not only the latest one.

\paragraph*{On-chain signal}
A funding transaction is built by the funder's wallet. On channels whose two endpoints share a
high-confidence label (so the funder's software is known), two wallet families separate cleanly: LND's
btcwallet (\texttt{nLockTime}~0, \texttt{nSequence}~\texttt{0x0}) and Core/BDK-style wallets (anti-fee-sniping
\texttt{nLockTime} at the current height, \texttt{nSequence}~\texttt{0xfffffffd}), used by CLN, Eclair (via
bitcoind) and software based on the Bitcoin Development Kit (BDK). Since chain data do not reveal which endpoint funded a channel, we score
each node with a likelihood ratio in which a channel is funded by the node with probability $\theta=0.5$ and
otherwise by the peer, whose wallet distribution follows the peer's label; batch opens, where one node is
common to every channel funded by the transaction, identify the funder exactly. The signal measures the
\emph{wallet}, not the daemon: LND operators who fund channels from an external wallet look Core-style. It
separates LND from the other clients but cannot tell CLN from Eclair or identify LDK (our LDK sample is 99\% one
operator).

\paragraph*{Validation}
Table~\ref{tab:classifier-eval} and Fig.~\ref{fig:validation} report two independent checks. Against 795 nodes
whose alias names their implementation (pooled over monthly snapshots; LND's default alias excluded because the
rules use it), precision and recall are 99.5\%/94\% for LND and 97\%/96\% for CLN. Most LND misses are nodes
without announced features, which the rules leave Unknown. Eclair (9 nodes, precision 62\%) and LDK (6) are too
few to grade; Eclair-labeled nodes that lack bit 37 may be CLN nodes with keysend disabled, so Eclair's node count is
an upper bound, although its capacity is dominated by ACINQ, which is attributed directly. Against the on-chain
wallet family (5-fold held out for high-confidence labels), nodes labeled by LND-only bits agree 99\% of the time
($n=3{,}199$) and the CLN set with keysend 98\% ($n=471$); the CLTV-only fallbacks are weakest (83--95\%) and account
for five of the nine alias disagreements (e.g.\ LND nodes of one node-in-a-box platform use CLTV~144, Eclair's
default). A learned baseline ($k$-nearest neighbours on the feature-bit vector) does not beat the rules on the same
held-out folds (macro-F1 0.92 vs.\ 0.93). Over all nodes with at least five funding transactions, held-out agreement
between the on-chain family and high-confidence labels is 99.2--99.3\%, and 98\% of nodes get a decision; because
the signal grades LND against everything else and most nodes are LND, the per-tier figures above are the more
informative ones.

\section{Concentration, 2023--2026}
\label{sec:concentration}

Figure~\ref{fig:share} shows monthly client shares of public nodes and of node capacity (each channel counted
for both endpoints). LND's share of capacity stayed between 84\% and 90\% throughout. Its share of nodes fell
from 91.8\% (May~2023) to 84.2\% (October~2026), but within each vantage epoch the net change is about one point
(91.8\% to 90.9\% in the first; in the second it moved between 82\% and 87\% and ended at 84.2\%, against
85.1\% at its start); the step between epochs coincides with the change of our gossip node and the
appearance of many featureless (Unknown) nodes, not with a change in the network. The only clear within-epoch
trend is LDK's capacity share, from 1.7\% (July~2024) to 5.4\%, mostly Block's routing nodes. On the latest
snapshot (Table~\ref{tab:client-census}) LND runs 84.0\% of nodes (77.5\% counting only high-confidence labels)
and holds 84.4\% of capacity; CLN runs 8.5\% of nodes but holds 3.7\% of capacity; Eclair's 43 nodes hold 6.1\%.
Capacity is highly unequal within every cohort (Gini 0.92--0.97). The largest nodes are, if anything, more
concentrated by client than nodes in general: LND runs 90 of the 100 largest nodes of the routable core
(\S\ref{sec:resilience}) and 8 of the 10 largest; the other two are ACINQ's Eclair node and Block's LDK node.

\section{The 2026 Security Wave and the CLN Shutdown}
\label{sec:outage}

\paragraph*{Timeline}
On 19~July a researcher disclosed two memory-exhaustion denial-of-service bugs in CLN, already
fixed~\cite{pratap2026clndos}. On 7~August BTCPay Server reported that its versions before 2.4.2 let remote
attackers obtain the LND credentials (macaroons) of merchant nodes and that funds had been
stolen~\cite{btcpay2026advisory}; affected merchants found channels force-closed and balances
swept~\cite{rubboli2026btcpay}. This was an application-layer credential leak, not an LND protocol bug. On
11~August Lightning Labs published ten LND advisories, three of high severity, all for versions before
0.19~\cite{lightninglabs2026aug}. On 26~August CLN maintainers, after weeks of triaging a high volume of
vulnerability reports, advised operators to upgrade or, if they could not, to restart with \texttt{--offline},
which keeps the node running and watching the chain but drops every peer connection; the advice circulated during
the day and the project posted it publicly at 23:26~UTC~\cite{snoffline2026,coreln2026offline}. CLN 26.06.7
shipped on 28~August~\cite{cln26067}, LDK 0.2.6 on 9~September~\cite{ldk026} and Eclair 0.14.3 on
14~September~\cite{eclair0143}. Lightning Labs published eight more advisories (four for LND, four for btcd) on
21~September~\cite{lightninglabs2026sep}, CLN 26.06.8 followed on 22~September~\cite{cln26068}, and on 2~October
CLN warned that attackers were targeting nodes running 26.06.7 or earlier~\cite{coreln2026attack}. Every
implementation was affected; the bugs were different.

\paragraph*{Measure}
For client $c$ and day $t$, $D_c(t)$ is the share of channel directions pointing \emph{toward} nodes of $c$ that
their peers mark disabled, unweighted and weighted by capacity. A node restarted with \texttt{--offline} and a node
that was shut down look the same to its peers. Client labels are fixed per node on 1~August~2026, so the incident
cannot reshuffle cohorts. The baseline is 1--25~August.

\paragraph*{Magnitude}
On 27~August $D_{\mathrm{CLN}}$ rose from a baseline of $11.8\pm0.9\%$ to 38.1\% ($z=29$); by capacity from
7.4\% to 35.2\% (Table~\ref{tab:outage-effect}, Fig.~\ref{fig:outage}). LND, Eclair and LDK did not move
($|z|<2$). A difference-in-differences estimate against LND over the following two weeks, with a moving-block
bootstrap, gives $+14.2$ percentage points (95\% CI 11.2--16.0) by channel count and $+14.7$ (10.4--17.1) by
capacity. Removing, each day, the single node with the most disabled capacity leaves $+12.9$ (8.9--15.2), so
the capacity result is not one large node (Table~\ref{tab:outage-robustness}).

\paragraph*{Who complied}
We call a node \emph{dark} when at least 80\% of the channel directions toward it are disabled, or when it is
absent from gossip. Of 624 CLN nodes not dark on 25~August, 137 (22.0\%) were dark on 27~August, holding 33\% of
CLN capacity. On an ordinary day the same transition (not dark on day $t-1$, dark on $t+1$) affects 2.3\% of CLN
nodes (median of 97 days, May--August; 95th percentile 5.6\%, maximum 12.3\%), so the call took about ten
times the usual number of CLN nodes off the network. LND nodes over the same days went dark at 6.4\%, within
their ordinary range (median 5.2\%, 95th percentile 9.5\%). Larger operators complied more: 32\% of the largest
capacity quintile against 8--27\% of the others (Table~\ref{tab:outage-compliance}). Compliance split by how nodes
are reachable: 28\% ($n=310$) of Tor-only CLN nodes went dark, 21\% ($n=150$) of dual-stack nodes and 7\% ($n=90$) of
clearnet-only nodes. Toward Tor-only CLN nodes the disabled share rose from 15.7\% to 39.0\%, toward dual-stack
nodes from 6.9\% to 47.4\%, while toward clearnet-only nodes it rose from 9.7\% to 19.0\% for two days and the
two-week difference-in-differences is zero (Table~\ref{tab:outage-robustness}). Tor-only LND nodes did not move
(25.6\% baseline, 23.4\% on 27~August), so this was not a Tor outage. A plausible reading, which our data cannot
confirm, is that home and node-in-a-box operators, typically on Tor, followed the call, and hosted operators
upgraded instead.

\paragraph*{A transport-level contrast}
Gossip also shows a common-mode failure that was \emph{not} client-correlated. On 10~July the disabled share toward
Tor-only nodes of every client rose from 23.7\% to 43.5\% overnight (clearnet-only nodes flat) and decayed over two
weeks (Fig.~\ref{fig:tor}). It coincides with a denial-of-service wave against the Tor network, in which the number
of relays serving onion-service descriptors fell from 3,874 on 9~July to 1,780 on 12~July and recovered only after
22~July~\cite{aeo2026doswave,aeo2026hsdir,torcollector2026}; Tor-only Lightning nodes are reachable only through
those descriptors. Common-mode failures follow transport as well as software.

\paragraph*{Recovery}
Of the 137 nodes, 39\% were back within three days and 53\% within a week; among nodes that returned, the median
time dark was three days (interquartile range 3--7), so most returns came shortly after the release of CLN 26.06.7
on 28~August, consistent with operators upgrading and reconnecting (gossip does not reveal versions). Twenty-two
percent never reappeared, even for a day, by 4~October; counting nodes that came back and went dark again, 32\% were
dark on that date (Fig.~\ref{fig:recovery}). The 2~October warning produced no comparable second wave
($D_{\mathrm{CLN}}$ 14.6\% on 2~October, 15.3\% on 4~October).

\paragraph*{Closing behaviour and confounds}
Read naively, closing data are full of ``incidents''. The two largest force-close days in the window were
single operators: on 3~August one LND node (NiceHash) was party to 226 force closes, and on 26~August---the day
of the CLN call---another LND node (Wallet of Satoshi), whose operator had announced a move away from its custodial
Lightning wallet~\cite{bitcoinworld2026wos}, was party to 205. The early-August cooperative-close wave was driven by
large operators closing hundreds of channels each: Kraken, which moved to a new node on
12~August~\cite{kraken2026node}, was party to 12.6\% of all closes on 2--16~August. We therefore separate
\emph{mass-close events}, a node party to at least 20 force closes in a UTC day (37 events since May, 30 by LND
nodes), from organic force closes. Organic force closes of channels involving a CLN node ran at 2.2 times their
July--August rate during 26~August--8~September, 2.6 times relative to channels between two LND nodes, whose rate
fell slightly (0.83 times; Table~\ref{tab:outage-compliance}). Within CLN, the channels of nodes that went dark were
force-closed at 70 per thousand channels in those two weeks, against 43 for CLN nodes that stayed up.
An \texttt{--offline} node can still broadcast its own commitment transactions, and chain data do not reveal which
side broadcast a close, so we cannot attribute these closes to peers. Force closes are not a sign of theft: we find
17 justice spends in 21 months, the last on 11~July~2026; a successful theft leaves none, so this bounds attempts
only from below.

\paragraph*{A contracting network}
The security wave coincided with the largest contraction of the public graph in our data, and the contraction was
not client-specific. Of the 38,081 channels in gossip on 1~August, 14,547 (38\%, holding 44\% of the capacity) were
gone by 4~October; 97\% of them closed on-chain, 10,464 in August, against 6,833 new channels. In the window of the
same length starting 1~May, 18\% of channels disappeared. Channels involving LND, CLN, Eclair and LDK nodes
disappeared at similar rates (38\%, 42\%, 32\% and 48\%); 79\% of the closes were cooperative, and ten operators,
led by Kraken's node migration, were party to 31\% of them. Exits by large operators, not client failures, account
for the network's shrinkage.

\paragraph*{Cost}
The median force close since May cost 1,222~sat in fees including the sweeps of its outputs, six times a
cooperative close (209~sat); all 8,787 force closes paid 0.17~BTC in total. The on-chain cost of the episode was
negligible; its cost was liquidity locked behind time-locks and channels lost.

\section{What Decides the Blast Radius}
\label{sec:resilience}

\paragraph*{Model}
We take the routable core of the public graph on 25~August~2026, the day before the call: channels whose two
directions are announced and enabled (73\% of announced channels; 4,435 nodes). A payment of $a$ satoshis can
use a channel whose capacity is at least $a$ (optimistic) or $2a$ (balances split evenly). Failures are modelled as
\emph{exposure}, a fraction $p$ of a client's nodes being unavailable, not deletion of the client. Our main
measure is $R_{\mathrm{all}}(a)$, the share of the pre-failure node pairs still connected by a path of usable
channels, so pairs involving a failed node count as lost; we also report $R(a)$ among surviving nodes and
$R_c(a)$ among surviving pairs of client $c$. Connectivity is an upper bound on what routing achieves (no fees, no
in-flight HTLCs, no liquidity uncertainty beyond the threshold). Random scenarios average 50 draws. Eclair has
only 12 nodes in the core, so $R_{\mathrm{Eclair}}$ moves in large steps.

\paragraph*{The observed footprint against ordinary churn}
The observed footprint is the set of nodes of the 25~August core with no usable channel left on 27~August (383
nodes, 105 of them CLN). It reduced $R_{\mathrm{all}}$ for 100k-sat payments by 7.8 points. We compute the same
two-day footprint for every pair of snapshots two days apart from May to August (Fig.~\ref{fig:placebo}). In
94 windows without a known incident the loss had a median of 3.6 points; 4 of them lost more
than the CLN shutdown. For pairs of CLN nodes, counting pairs with a failed member as lost, the outage cost 24.5
points; no ordinary window cost more than 17.7 (median 3.2). The two windows spanning the Tor
event of 10~July cost the whole network 15.6--16.0 points, twice the CLN shutdown.

\paragraph*{Scenarios}
Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} and Fig.~\ref{fig:curves} compare failures. The calibrated scenario ($\hat p=0.22$ of
CLN nodes failing at random, 136 nodes) leaves $R$ among survivors at 78.8\%, against 77.9\% when the same number
of nodes fails at random across all clients: for the rest of the network, a CLN-wide outage behaves like random
failure, because CLN nodes are mostly peripheral. Drawing the failures with the observed compliance of each size
quintile and transport class instead gives the same result ($R=78.7\%$, $R_{\mathrm{all}}=73.9\%$). The damage stays inside the cohort: counting
pairs with a failed member as lost, CLN pairs fall from 79\% to 46\% connected, while among surviving CLN nodes
$R_{\mathrm{CLN}}$ is 76\%. Removing the same number of nodes chosen by degree leaves 27.6\%, by capacity 33.7\%.
Losing all 619 CLN nodes of the core leaves survivors at 76.2\%; losing its 12 Eclair nodes, which hold 5.8\% of
capacity and are dominated by ACINQ, leaves 63.9\%. Per unit of failed capacity, failures confined to Eclair are the
most damaging and failures confined to LDK the least (Fig.~\ref{fig:curves}).

\paragraph*{Hubs, not cohorts}
Single nodes explain the gap between cohorts. Removing each of the 300 largest nodes of the core on its own, the
most critical by far is ACINQ's Eclair node: it holds 4.9\% of node capacity, and its loss alone cuts $R$ by 15.9
points, four times the loss from all 619 CLN nodes (3.7 points). The next most critical nodes, a Lightning service
provider and a payment processor running LND, cost 2.8 and 1.9 points; the 20 most critical nodes, 19 of them LND,
cost a median of 0.6 points each. Criticality follows a node's role rather than its size: the two largest nodes by
capacity, both exchanges, are not among the six most critical, whereas nodes that are the only adequate link for
many small nodes are. The software of these few nodes, not the client shares of Table~\ref{tab:client-census},
decides how far a client-correlated failure reaches.

\paragraph*{Dependence on LND, against a null model}
Minority clients' channel capacity faces LND: 76\% for CLN, 92\% for Eclair and 81\% for LDK (Fig.~\ref{fig:matrix}),
and without LND nodes only 32\% of CLN pairs, 42\% of Eclair pairs and 11\% of LDK pairs remain connected for
100k-sat payments (14\%, 42\% and 9\% for 1M sats). Read alone, this suggests that minority clients reach each other
only through LND. A null model says otherwise. Permuting client labels among nodes of similar capacity (within
capacity deciles, 200 draws) keeps the graph and the cohort sizes and removes any preference of a client for its own
kind. Under it, CLN would put 88\% of its capacity on LND peers and LDK 87\%; both put less. CLN nodes
keep 15\% of their capacity among CLN peers against 8\% by chance, and LDK 15\% against 2\%. Without
LND, 9\% of CLN pairs would remain connected by chance (95th percentile 25\%), against 32\% observed; for
Eclair and LDK the observed values lie within the null range. Minority clients' dependence on LND is what their size
implies; CLN operators in fact build more of their own backbone than chance would, but it is too small to stand
alone.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph*{Containment, not collapse}
The first measured client-wide outage in the LN was absorbed. The network as a whole lost about as much
reachability as in an unusual but ordinary two days, and half as much as in the Tor denial-of-service wave a month
earlier, while the affected cohort lost a third of its internal reachability and saw organic force closes double.
Client-correlated failure was costly for the client's own users, not for everyone else, because the nodes that
complied were peripheral.

\paragraph*{Where the risk is}
The blast radius of a correlated failure is set by its overlap with the hubs. A few large nodes, regardless of
client, carry the network's reachability; ACINQ alone outweighs the entire CLN cohort for the survivors. Node-count
diversity statistics therefore mislead in both directions: CLN's 8.5\% of nodes protected little, and LND's 84\%
matters mainly through the subset of LND nodes that are hubs. The structure that diversity statistics hide is not a
pathological dependence of minority clients on LND, which is what their size implies, but the concentration of
connectivity in a few operators, whose software choice is a single point of failure for everyone.

\paragraph*{Operational lessons}
Compliance with an emergency call was voluntary and uneven (22\% of CLN nodes, mostly Tor-reachable); a third of
those were still dark after five weeks, so emergency shutdowns have lasting costs for the operators who follow them.
Advising \texttt{--offline} rather than a shutdown was the safer design, because an offline node keeps watching the
chain for revoked states and expiring HTLCs. Single-operator events (mass closes, an exchange migrating its node, a
custodial wallet winding down) are easily mistaken for network-wide effects; concentration checks belong in any
incident analysis. Gossip pipelines should validate dumps (we found saved error responses) and record their vantage
point.

\paragraph*{Recommendations}
(i)~Monitor peer-side disabled shares per client and per transport as an early-warning signal; it is cheap and
was unambiguous for both the Tor and the CLN events. (ii)~Report client diversity weighted by hub capacity or
reachability, not by node count. (iii)~Encourage channels between minority-client hubs, so that CLN, Eclair and LDK
users are not connected only through LND. (iv)~Scope emergency guidance by version and duration, and prefer
\texttt{--offline} to shutdown.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
Our gossip is one node's view of the announced graph, which changed in mid-2024 and has gaps before 2026.
Unannounced channels (most LDK and Phoenix usage) are invisible; their users reach the network through their
providers' nodes (Phoenix users through ACINQ's), so hub failures cost payments more than \S\ref{sec:resilience}
measures. Feature-bit rules can be wrong for new releases and
are weak where nodes announce no features; Eclair's node count is an upper bound. The on-chain signal identifies
wallets, not daemons, and cannot separate CLN from Eclair or identify LDK. Disabled flags record peers' views with
some delay, and a single vantage point can miss or delay updates; such gaps are not specific to one client, and
LND, Eclair and LDK, observed through the same node, did not move on 27~August. A channel between two CLN nodes
that both went dark is never disabled, so $D_{\mathrm{CLN}}$
understates the outage. The dark threshold (80\%) and the two-day footprint are choices; the churn baselines and
placebo windows use the same definitions. The resilience model measures connectivity under static capacity
thresholds, an upper bound on payment success. Channel balances are private, so we do not model how payments
rerouted around failed nodes drain liquidity on the surviving paths, nor fees, HTLC limits or private routing hints.
The absolute reachability levels therefore overstate what payments achieve; the observed outage, the placebo
windows and every scenario are measured with the same model, so the comparisons between them are like for like.
Chain data do not reveal which side broadcast a force close. Event dates are those of public announcements; some
advice circulated earlier in community channels.

\section{Ethics}
We use only public gossip and blockchain data and report aggregates. We name large public operators only for
their closing behaviour, which is public on-chain and in the operators' own announcements, and for ACINQ's widely
known role as a hub; we describe other critical nodes by type and publish no lists of potentially vulnerable nodes.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
LND holds about 85\% of public Lightning capacity, a share that has barely moved in three years. When an emergency
took a fifth of CLN nodes offline in August~2026, the network absorbed it: the damage stayed within the CLN cohort,
and a transport failure a month earlier had cost the network twice as much. The fragility of the network lies not in
how many nodes run each client but in which software its few hubs run. Client diversity protects the Lightning
Network to the extent that it reaches those hubs.
